SALLM Advisor Brief: Why xLSTM is strong overall, but weak on NER and T2X

Evidence pack for the advisor meeting. The goal is to explain mechanisms with exact examples, not just report scores. Test-set results are used where available; validation/diagnostic evidence is labelled as mechanism evidence.

xLSTM is genuinely useful after task-specific adaptation.

News, POS, and multilingual intent show real learning. The strong result is not a spreadsheet artefact, but it is strongest for closed-label or dense token-aligned tasks.

xLSTM's weak spots are copying and structured generation.

NER span extraction and T2X require exact source binding. xLSTM often learns labels, but loses spans, boundaries, or relation frames.

Mamba's POS/NER failure is more fundamental.

It fails even in simplified tag-generation diagnostics, so the issue is not just official parser strictness or one bad prompt.

Literature caveat: the precise claim “xLSTM is especially strong at POS” is our empirical finding, not yet a widely established xLSTM result. It is consistent with older LSTM-family sequence-labeling work, where BiLSTM/CRF-style models were strong on POS and NER because they model dense token-label sequences. But those papers often used bidirectional encoders and CRF decoding, while our setup is decoder-only generative xLSTM, so we should phrase this as: “our xLSTM result is consistent with the historical strength of LSTM-family models on dense token labeling, but the xLSTM-specific evidence here is our experiment.”

3. Why xLSTM Does Not Also Win SIB200

SIB is also classification, but it is not the same kind of classification as News or InjongoIntent. It uses short topic labels and forced-choice log-likelihood scoring, and one label, science/technology, is a long slash-separated surface form. The examples show xLSTM often assigns obvious science texts to short labels such as health, travel, or sports.

It often avoids the long science/technology label even for clear science texts. This is a different failure mode from NER copying: SIB is mostly label-surface/log-likelihood calibration plus topic semantics.

4. Why POS Works For xLSTM But NER Does Not

POS burden

  • Dense supervision: every token receives a tag.
  • Closed label set: tags like VERB, NOUN, PROPN.
  • Parser can recover a token-tag table even if raw generation is not elegant.

NER burden

  • Sparse supervision: most tokens are non-entities.
  • Open copying: the model must reproduce exact surface spans.
  • Small boundary, delimiter, label, repetition, or hallucination errors directly reduce F1.

5. Why Mamba Fails On POS And NER

Mamba's problem is not just strict parsing. It fails when asked to produce normal spans, and it also fails simplified tag sequences.

How to answer the HPO objection: the raw POS example by itself could be explained by poor decoding, prompt formatting, or HPO. The stronger claim comes from the follow-up controls: when we forced legal, exact-length tag outputs, calibrated away all-O NER collapse, and ran full-data atomic tag-sequence rescue, Mamba still failed to recover reliable token-label alignment. So the result is not “we never tuned it”; it is “under this 125M HF pure-Mamba2 setup, the failure survived the obvious output-contract and HPO controls.”

9. Should We Just HPO xLSTM For NER And AfriHG?

Short answer: We should not run broad blind HPO and hope it becomes excellent. The evidence supports small, hypothesis-driven HPO/rescue gates. NER needs a copy/span-structure intervention; AfriHG needs a headline-planning/decoding intervention. If those gates move validation and examples, then we scale to official test.
Task What we already tried What the result says Best next HPO shape
xLSTM NER Four official span-generation HPO variants plus a decoder-only token-aligned diagnostic. Completed HPO variants scored around 0.127-0.141 F1, below the earlier mono Xhosa NER range around 0.20-0.25. Ordinary LoRA/LR/epoch HPO did not solve the bottleneck. The diagnostic showed nonzero entity-label learning (0.176 validation entity-tag F1/non-O recall), so the weakness is span copying/formatting, not complete inability to learn entity labels. Structural HPO only: tag-sequence-to-span conversion, placeholder/delexicalized spans, constrained span templates, or copy-aware output formatting. Success means beating the earlier official mono range and showing cleaner copied spans in examples.
xLSTM AfriHG Mono AfriHG was weak: Xhosa chrF about 16.59, Zulu chrF about 17.27, both BLEU 0. Focus-prompt validation canary completed and was treated as negative: outputs remained semantically weak rather than clearly better headlines. This is less obviously hopeless than NER because AfriHG is a generation-quality task where decoding/checkpoint selection sometimes helps. But prompt-only focus did not magically fix semantic headline conditioning. A small AfriHG-specific decode/checkpoint-selection sweep is reasonable, especially beam/length penalty and repetition controls, but only if evaluated with examples. A broad retraining HPO should wait until the current xLSTM 0-shot AfriHG job finishes and we compare against LLaMA/Mamba official test rows.
Meeting phrasing: “We should optimize xLSTM where the failure is plausibly recipe-sensitive, but we should not treat HPO as a magic explanation. For NER, the evidence points to a structural span-copy problem, so ordinary hyperparameter sweeps already looked negative. For AfriHG, a bounded decoding/checkpoint-selection sweep is still defensible, but the success criterion must be semantic headline quality plus official test metrics, not just a small chrF bump.”
